# The interview

## Why it is shaped like this

The interview is the reason this skill produces a different site each time instead of one template
with different colours. Four things make a scroll-driven page work, and none of them can be inferred
from a business name: the **order** the reader meets things, the **one belief** they should leave
with, which **assets are real**, and the **signature move** that makes the page memorable. Ask for
those and nothing else.

Ask about feeling and motive, not features. "What sections do you want?" gets you a sitemap —
About, Services, Contact — and a sitemap is exactly the generic page they are trying to escape.
"What must they believe by the end?" gets you a spine.

Keep it to two rounds of at most four questions. People abandon long forms, and you can infer more
than you think once you have the spine. Use `AskUserQuestion` if available — concrete options make
people decide instead of writing an essay — and plain numbered questions otherwise. Always propose
your own recommendation as the first option, based on what you found in Phase 0; a lot of users will
just take it, and that is a good outcome.

## Round one — the spine

**1. What does the reader hit first, and in what order after that?**

Offer three or four orderings built from what actually exists, each with its trade-off named. For
example, for a community with strong numbers, a founder story and screenshots of wins:

- *Proof first* — open on the receipts (numbers, screenshots), then the story. Strongest for a cold
  stranger who has no reason to trust you yet.
- *Story first* — open on the founder, then the proof. Strongest when the person is the brand.
- *Show, don't tell* — open inside the product/room/event, then explain it. Strongest when the thing
  is visually interesting on its own.

Say which you would pick and why. The answer becomes the scene order in the score.

**2. What must the reader believe by the end? One sentence, not a feature list.**

This is the most load-bearing answer in the interview. Everything on the page either serves that
sentence or is cut. If they give you a feature list anyway, offer a one-sentence version back and
ask if it is right — people recognise their own belief faster than they can write it.

Push back if the sentence is a slogan with no content ("we're the best in the industry"). A usable
answer has a subject and a claim: "that this is where serious people go to learn this, and that it
works."

**3. What assets do you actually have?**

Be specific about what you found in Phase 0 and ask what is missing. Photos, screenshots, logo,
video, brand colours, a live site to pull copy from. This single answer decides how much of Phase 3
is generation versus curation, and generated filler in place of real material is the difference
between a site that feels like a company and one that feels like a mockup.

Ask explicitly whether they *want* generated imagery, and in what register — stylised and abstract,
diagrammatic, or pseudo-photographic. Volunteer that stylised holds up better, because generated
"real photos" of people and places are where the uncanny valley lives.

**4. One thing this page should do that no page you have seen does.**

This becomes the signature move — the thing a visitor describes to someone else afterwards. If they
have no idea, propose two or three candidates from what they have already told you. Good signature
moves are usually a mechanism made visible, not an effect: every claim carrying a source that
unlocks as you scroll; a rail of dated receipts; one product held in focus while the rest recede;
a map filling in as a counter climbs.

## Round two — the feel

Only ask these if round one left them genuinely open. Two or three is plenty.

**Where should it feel calm, and where intense?** This is the rhythm, and it prevents both failure
modes — the page that shouts for 900vh and the page that never raises its voice.

**How much motion is too much?** Anchor the options in recognisable references rather than
adjectives: restrained-editorial (motion only where it explains), moderate (two or three scrubbed
scenes), or maximal (near-continuous response to scroll). Mismatched motion appetite is the single
most common source of "I don't love it" after the first draft.

**Brand constraints.** Colours, fonts, logo usage, tone of voice, and anything that must not change.
Ask directly whether existing copy is sacred or rewritable — rewriting copy they were attached to
wastes a whole round.

**Where must the page send people?** Real destinations and real URLs for every call to action. A
button that goes nowhere, or to the wrong page, is the most annoying possible defect to find later
and it is free to prevent now.

## When nobody answers

Sometimes there is no human in the loop at all: an automated run, a brief forwarded without a
contact, a client who stopped replying. The interview still does its work — you just answer it
yourself.

Take each question in turn and decide it from the brief, the existing site, and the industry. Record
every answer in the score as a **decision**, not as a fact: "Reihenfolge: Proof first — angenommen,
weil die Zielgruppe kalt ist" reads very differently from a claim that the client wanted that. Where
a decision could plausibly have gone the other way, name the alternative in one clause, so a later
reader sees the fork rather than only the road taken.

Then keep building. A page resting on four recorded assumptions beats no page, and it gives the
client something concrete to react to — people correct a draft far more readily than they answer a
questionnaire. Close the handover by listing the decisions that were yours, ordered by how much the
page would change if they are wrong. That list is the agenda for the first real conversation.

The one thing that does not become an assumption is a fact about the world: a number, a customer, a
testimonial, a date. Those stay out until someone supplies them. Assuming a structure is design;
assuming a statistic is fabrication.

## The blank slate variant

When there is no business, no assets and no copy — someone exploring, or building a portfolio piece
— do not run the full interview. Ask two questions: what the thing is, and what feeling it should
have. Then invent a coherent, clearly fictional brand (name, palette, voice, three plausible claims)
and say plainly that the numbers and testimonials are placeholders. Announcing the fiction once, up
front, keeps it from later being mistaken for real proof.

## After the interview

Restate what you heard in four or five lines — order, belief, assets, signature move, motion
appetite — and get a yes. A single wrong assumption here costs an entire build cycle, and people
correct a short summary readily while they will not re-read a long one.

Then write the scroll score (Phase 2). Do not start coding from the interview directly; the score is
where you find out that scene four says the same thing as scene two.
